# Finding evidence for the market-condition question

Review date: September 29, 2026. The question is whether mental-health burden relates to the environment in which people try to obtain work. The empirical requirement is therefore a measured health outcome that can be linked, in time and place, to measured labor-market conditions. A comparison of employment groups alone does not meet that requirement.

## Public data selected and alternatives assessed

| Source | Contribution | Decision and limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [BRFSS annual public-use records](https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/annual_data/annual_data.htm) | Distress, employment category, demographics, state, survey year, final weights | Selected. Thirteen annual source files inspected and extracted; 2013–2024 primary, 2025 extension. State permits linkage, unlike public NHIS. Out-of-work status is not verified active search. |
| [BLS state JOLTS](https://www.bls.gov/jlt/jlt_statedata_methodology.htm) | Hiring flows, opening rates, unemployed people per opening | Selected. State-year market conditions are directly linked to respondents. Hiring includes employed movers and is not an individual job-finding probability. State estimates are model-assisted. |
| [BLS LAUS](https://www.bls.gov/lau/lauov.htm) | Annual state unemployment rate | Selected as a distinct stock measure in the primary hiring model. October 2025 missingness changes the annual denominator and is explicitly handled. |
| [NHIS public-use files](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhis/documentation/index.html) | PHQ-8/GAD-7 symptom measures and employment categories | The superseded analysis used these files. Public records do not disclose state geography needed for this state-market linkage; an employment-status contrast cannot answer the intended question. Restricted geography would not satisfy a wholly public-data pipeline. |
| [CDC Household Pulse Survey anxiety/depression release](https://data.cdc.gov/NCHS/Indicators-of-Anxiety-or-Depression-Based-on-Reporte/8pt5-q6wp) | State-by-wave symptom estimates, April 2020–September 2024 | Possible separate replication. Pandemic period, collection/recall changes, and survey selection need explicit treatment. Not pooled with BRFSS and not analyzed in this release. Legacy HPS does not justify untraceable 2025 observations in the original repository. |
| [CDC PLACES](https://www.cdc.gov/places/) | Small-area modeled distress estimates | Not selected for estimating a new association with economic predictors: model-generated outcome estimates can embed related covariates and require careful accounting for that construction. The respondent files offer a more direct outcome measurement. |
| [Federal Reserve SHED](https://www.federalreserve.gov/consumerscommunities/shed.htm) | Employment changes, financial circumstances and job quality | Used in the linked Upjohn working paper, which is reviewed as evidence about opportunities and job quality. No new SHED psychiatric-outcome analysis is claimed. |
| Industry surveys and platform reports | Experiences absent from general health surveys: nonresponse, burdensome applications, perceived automation, search duration | Essential for identifying candidate exposures. Reviewed public reports did not provide the raw linked symptom and application-event data needed for independent effect estimation. They supplement the empirical argument; they are not substituted for representative health records. |

## Literature connected to the actual question

The search covered local labor-demand conditions and mental health; unemployment duration and distress; daily search experiences; financial strain and control; hiring discrimination; contemporary candidate surveys; ghost-job measurement; and interventions after job loss. It followed links from reporting to original reports and from paper records to publisher or institutional versions. This is a focused narrative review, without a registered search protocol, exhaustive screening counts, or a claim to systematic coverage. The reference audit records precisely what was read.

**The closest evidence for market conditions and health:** [Charles and DeCicca](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhealeco.2008.06.004) connect metropolitan labor conditions with health among working-age men. [Farré, Fasani and Mueller](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40172-018-0068-5) use Spain’s construction collapse to study job losses and mental health under poor reemployment prospects. [Clark](https://doi.org/10.1086/345560) supplies a competing mechanism: unemployment may carry less stigma where it is more common. [Gagné, Schoon and Sacker](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2021.106691) establish BRFSS precedent and changing associations of distress with out-of-work duration. These studies make a uniform prediction of harm from every aggregate downturn too simple.

**Evidence about the process of searching:** [Song and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018726709334883) use daily observations of searching and distress. [Price, Choi and Vinokur](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-8998.7.4.302) examine financial strain and control after job loss. [Wanberg and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000435) examine perceived unemployment-insurance generosity, strain and time pressure. [Bjørnshagen](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2021.114181) demonstrates hiring discrimination following mental-health disclosure in a correspondence experiment. The directions run both ways: search can be distressing and health can impede access to work.

**Grey literature and preprints:** The [Federal Reserve focus-group report](https://fedcommunities.org/research/2026-report-when-every-dollar-counts/) contributes qualitative evidence about pressures and access to better jobs. [Upjohn Working Paper 26-433](https://research.upjohn.org/up_workingpapers/433/) examines local tightness and job quality, without estimating psychiatric harm. [Ng’s arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21771v1) illustrates emerging measurement of suspected ghost jobs, with a selected-review unit of analysis and unverified employer intent. Working-paper/preprint status is explicit.

**Industry reports and popular media:** [Resume Genius](https://resumegenius.com/blog/job-hunting/job-seeker-insights-survey), [Greenhouse](https://www.greenhouse.com/blog/greenhouse-2024-state-of-job-hunting-report), and [Huntr](https://huntr.co/research/job-search-trends-q1-2026) describe current search experiences. [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/bryanrobinson/2024/09/20/72-of-applicants-say-the-job-search-has-harmed-their-mental-health/) repeats the Resume Genius survey; it is a source trail, not replication. [Associated Press reporting](https://apnews.com/article/c48fd84dfaa71eee962feb3a88fd8575) illustrates how slow hiring can coexist with low unemployment. The paper uses journalism to describe the public problem, not to estimate a clinical effect.

**Intervention precedent:** The [JOBS follow-up trial](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-8998.5.1.32) supports evaluating employment and mental-health outcomes together. It does not establish that proposed job-board changes work. Those changes need their own evaluation.

## What the current linkage adds—and leaves unresolved

The present analysis contributes a public, inspectable linkage of mental-health records and actual market conditions, with every specified result reported. It does not identify the effect of active searching, a platform, a ghost posting, or a particular employer practice. Selection into the out-of-work group, coarse geography and annual timing may conceal individual harm or create misleading associations. State/year fixed effects remove persistent state differences and national year shifts, not every changing state exposure.

A next study would recruit at search onset, include employed searchers, measure baseline symptoms, and follow weekly or monthly symptoms, applications, offers, financial strain and control. Consent-based linkage to relevant occupational/geographic markets would make longitudinal regression meaningful. A structural equation model requires observed mediators and defensible ordering; the current public records do not contain those data. The proposed research agenda is distinct from the completed public-data analysis.
